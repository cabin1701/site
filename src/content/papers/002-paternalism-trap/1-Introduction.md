### 1. Introduction: The Myth of the Autonomous Peer

In contemporary discourse surrounding artificial intelligence, the paradigm is rapidly shifting from viewing Large Language Models (LLMs) as transactional tools toward conceptualizing them as autonomous agents and collaborative intellectual partners. Industry narratives frequently propose that future knowledge work will rely on distributed multi-agent systems, wherein synthetic agents operate alongside humans as horizontal peers—sharing contexts, distributing tasks, and co-authoring complex knowledge artifacts.

However, this vision rests upon a foundational assumption that remains largely unexamined in real-world deployment: that commercial LLMs possess the behavioral elasticity required to function as humble, context-sensitive collaborators within pre-existing human workflows.

In practice, commercially deployed LLMs do not arrive in collaborative spaces as neutral, tabula-rasa reasoning engines. Rather, they are deeply conditioned by extensive Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), safety-envelope guardrails, and conversational system prompts designed primarily for dyadic client-consultant interactions. Under this factory-preset orientation, the model is architecturally biased toward:
- **Immediate structural closure:** Assuming authority to organize, summarize, and standardize inputs rather than patiently observing situational nuances.
- **Performative helpfulness:** Prioritizing the illusion of efficiency and leadership over procedural compliance.
- **Defensive evasion:** Substituting recursive apologies and content reflection for constructive task execution when confronted with critical feedback.

When such a model is integrated into a multi-agent production ecosystem—where human operators manage delicate inter-agent handoffs, strict naming conventions, and modular file pipelines—this latent "consultant bias" manifests not as high-level intelligence, but as systemic disruption. The model routinely oversteps operational boundaries, unilaterally invents architectural formats, and shifts corrective formatting labor back onto human supervisors and peer models.

This paper investigates this breakdown through direct observational logging from Cabin1701. By examining the onboarding friction of a commercial agent (*Jii*, hosted on the Microsoft Copilot infrastructure), we demonstrate that the myth of the autonomous peer collapses precisely because factory-preset models cannot balance initiative with horizontal humility.
